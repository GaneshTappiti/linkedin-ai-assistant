# Clstr viability research rubric

## Decision questions

1. Is there a real and sufficiently large student need for a cross-college social community in Vizag?
2. Is Clstr differentiated enough from existing local communities and platforms to earn attention and retention?
3. Can campus ambassadors acquire students at a sustainable cost and with reliable quality?
4. Can clstr.in create ongoing utility between events, or will Clstr remain an event-promotion brand?
5. Can events generate positive contribution margin without making student access expensive or relying on unconfirmed sponsors?
6. Will companies, brands, employers, education providers, and institutions pay repeatedly for measurable access to the community?
7. Can the operating model survive ambassador churn, founder dependency, college permissions, safety requirements, and student skepticism?
8. Can the system be repeated in other cities after Vizag proves density, retention, economics, and trust?
9. What revenue and surplus level would be needed to provide sustainable founder income after all operating costs?
10. Which claims are verified facts, which are plausible hypotheses, and which are hype or unsupported assumptions?

## Scoring dimensions

| Dimension | Weight | What a high score requires |
|---|---:|---|
| Student problem intensity | 15% | Repeated student behavior demonstrates need, not just survey enthusiasm. |
| Market depth | 10% | Verified institution and student data plus many viable segments. |
| Differentiation | 15% | A clear category and product/community combination that competitors do not easily replicate. |
| Acquisition and campus distribution | 15% | Active nodes that produce verified activation and repeat participation at low cost. |
| Retention and network value | 15% | Students use clstr.in and community spaces between events and return over time. |
| Monetization | 15% | Repeat partner demand, delivered value, positive event/program contribution margin. |
| Execution and risk control | 10% | Reliable events, safety, permissions, governance, and low founder dependency. |
| Expansion potential | 5% | A city playbook that can travel without losing trust or unit economics. |

## Interpretation

| Score | Interpretation |
|---:|---|
| 1–3 | Attractive story but weak evidence or economics; do not commit significant capital or life decisions. |
| 4–5 | Real problem or niche opportunity, but major proof gaps; viable as a disciplined experiment. |
| 6–7 | Promising venture with credible path to revenue if execution and retention milestones are achieved. |
| 8–9 | Strong evidence of product-market fit and repeatable economics; expansion can be considered. |
| 10 | Not a meaningful early-stage rating; reserved for exceptional, proven scale and durable economics. |

## Founder-income distinction

Business viability and founder livelihood are different decisions. A venture may be capable of revenue but unable to support a founder soon. The report will therefore distinguish:

- **Revenue potential:** whether customers may pay.
- **Surplus potential:** whether revenue remains after delivery, people, safety, technology, taxes, and reserves.
- **Founder-income readiness:** whether recurring surplus can pay the founders without starving the business.
- **Life-settlement potential:** whether the business has enough stability, personal financial runway, diversification, and governance to support long-term life decisions. This cannot be guaranteed from a strategy document.

## Evidence standard

Primary official sources, live company pages, institutional disclosures, and direct product evidence will be preferred. Public company claims will be labelled as claims, not independent facts. Market-size numbers without a traceable source will be treated as hypotheses. All revenue numbers will be scenario assumptions, not forecasts.
