I analyzed the traffic data for clstr.in across 3 engineering colleges last month.

The results completely changed how we design our product.

85% of student traffic is mobile.
Only 13% is desktop, and less than 2% is tablet.

Most web apps are designed desktop-first, then squeezed into mobile viewports.
But in a college campus, desktop-first design is a relic. 

Students use apps while walking between classes, sitting in the cafeteria, or waiting for buses.

This data forced three strict design rules for our team:

1. Thumb-zone navigation: Every primary action (like search, post, and menu) must be within 1.5 inches of the screen bottom. No top-left hamburger menus.
2. Lightweight PWA: Zero heavy frameworks. The app must load in under 1.5 seconds on a spotty 3G campus network.
3. Bottom sheets > modals: Traditional center popups feel broken on mobile. We use sliding bottom sheets for all interactions.

If you are building for students, stop looking at your desktop localhost. 

Build for the device they actually hold in their hand.

Have you shifted to mobile-first development, or are you still designing desktop-first? 👇

Cursor starts writing buggy code as your project grows.

Why? Context bloat.

Most developers dump their entire codebase into the prompt window.
The context gets bloated, matching rules break, and Cursor starts overwriting imports and hallucinating outdated syntax.

You spend more time debugging than shipping.

Here is the "Scope & Symbol" protocol I use to build clstr.in:

1. Prune the window: Stop using `@Workspace` for specific tasks. Reference only the exact file you are modifying and its corresponding type definition file.
2. Use `.cursorrules`: Add a custom rules file in your root to lock down package versions and tech stack constraints.
3. Start fresh: Start a new chat thread every 3–5 prompts. Keeping the history short prevents memory pollution.

Keep your context window under 20% capacity, and the code quality goes up 10x.

How do you manage your AI editor’s context? Let me know. 👇

Here's how I validate startup ideas in college without writing a single line of code.

Most students get an idea, open VS Code, and start coding for 3 months. Then they launch, get 0 signups, and quit.

Here is the exact step-by-step loop I use instead (Mom Test style) 👇

1️⃣ Define the core pain: Write down the top 3 assumptions about why this problem exists.
2️⃣ Find 10 users: Go to campus canteens or college WhatsApp groups and ask them about their daily struggles.
3️⃣ Don't pitch: Ask what they currently do to solve it, not "would you buy this?"
4️⃣ The landing page check: Build a simple page with a waitlist. If they don't give their email, they won't give their money.
5️⃣ The friction test: Ask them to perform a small action (like joining a feedback call) to prove they care.

Before you write code, confirm the pain exists. If people won't talk to you, they won't buy from you.

Save this for your next project. How do you validate your ideas before building?
